Welmströmung

Welmströmung (n.) /ˈvɛlmˌʃtrøːmʊŋ/ A steady current of deliberate action.

1. Premises

Welmströmung is based on several findings about how people typically judge effort, choose between actions and direct attention. None of these findings are new, however it's in how they are combined and applied that Welmströmung is invoked.

1.1 Judgments are relative.

People judge magnitude against reference points, not in isolation. Introducing anchors above or below a series of stimuli shifts how every item in the series is rated [1]. Subjective effort appears to work similarly. One account proposes that the felt cost of a task reflects its value compared with the next-best use of the same mental resources [2]. Welm assumes the same holds within a list: how hard a task looks depends partly on what is shown next to it.

1.2 People avoid demand, and small frictions matter.

Given a choice between lines of action, people consistently prefer the less demanding one, often without being aware of it [3]. Small amounts of procedural friction also have large effects. In a field experiment, helping families complete a complex aid application substantially increased submission rates, while giving information alone had no significant effect [4]. Friction can work in either direction: removing it makes an action more likely, and adding it makes an action less likely.

1.3 Salient items capture attention.

Attention is drawn to whatever stands out most, even when it is irrelevant. In visual search, an item with a unique colour pulls attention away from a known target, and this persists with practice [5]. In an interface, the most visually distinct element is the one most likely to be acted on.

2. The to-do list problem

People spend little effort creating to-dos. Items are often written as brief cues rather than full descriptions, and many are never completed [6]. The same research found that people are not poor at prioritising. The difficulty lies in the effort needed to keep a task list working [6].

Adding an item takes a second. Completing it can take hours, and removing an item without completing it feels like a small failure. The list grows, older items sink out of view, and the list becomes a record of obligations rather than a plan.

This has a cognitive cost. Unfulfilled goals stay active in the mind and interfere with unrelated tasks. That interference can be removed by forming a specific plan for the goal, even before it is completed [7]. A list of vague, accumulated items keeps many goals active without planning any of them. A list of deliberately chosen, concrete actions works more like a set of plans.

A to-do list works when every item on it was deliberately chosen. It fails when it becomes a place where items accumulate by default.

3. Mechanisms

Welm turns the premises in Section 1 into three mechanisms. Each one is built into how the app works, not offered as a feature the user can choose to use.

3.1 Completion as the cost of addition.

The list has a fixed capacity. A new item can only be added once an existing one has been completed or removed. Adding becomes a decision rather than a reflex, which addresses the growth problem in Section 2 and keeps the number of open goals low [7]. It also changes where pressure falls. In an unbounded list, the backlog's weight is spread evenly. In Welm, the remaining items press on the task in focus, because finishing it is what makes room. The demand-avoidance tendency in 1.2 is directed toward completion instead of away from it.

3.2 Choice within a set.

Tasks are presented in small sets rather than as one continuous list, and the user chooses freely within the set. Decision time increases with the number of alternatives [8]. Choice overload, where large option sets lead to deferral and dissatisfaction, is contested as a general effect. A meta-analysis found it depends on specific conditions, including task difficulty, preference uncertainty and a goal of minimising effort [9]. Choosing what to do next often meets those conditions. Small sets reduce the cost of each choice, and comparison within a set (1.1) usually makes at least one task look manageable.

3.3 Reduced distraction.

Focus tasks are visually separated from the rest of the list and always appear first. Everything else is subdued. This uses attentional capture (1.3) deliberately: the most salient element on screen is always the one the user has chosen to work on [5].

4. The practice

The mechanisms describe how the app works. The practice describes how it is used: a four-step loop, run once a day.

  1. Write. When something comes up, it is recorded as a concrete action, or rejected on the spot. Vague or aspirational items are rejected at this point. A concrete action works as a plan, not just a reminder [7].
  2. Read. Once a day, the full list is read top to bottom. This should take under a minute. If it takes longer, the list is too long.
  3. Do. Work proceeds from the list. Completed items are marked as done, and completion is registered visibly.
  4. Resolve. Any item read without prompting a response is deleted.

The loop involves no sorting, tagging, prioritising or planning. The list stays small enough not to need them.

The loop is run at the same point each day so that it becomes habitual. In a study of real-world habit formation, behaviours repeated daily in a consistent context became more automatic along an asymptotic curve. The time to reach a plateau varied widely between people [10].

5. Design notes

5.1 A lean visual system for minimal stimulation.

Every visual element competes for attention [5], so the system's default is to remove elements rather than add them. The palette is restrained, one typeface family is used, and colour is kept for status and focus.

5.2 Highlighting and visibility.

Visibility is used as a functional signal. Focus tasks get full contrast, and other items are shown at reduced emphasis. The user can always tell where to look without reading the whole screen.

5.3 Friction: deliberate resistance to adding.

Most productivity software makes capture as fast as possible. Welm makes adding slightly slower on purpose. Small procedural costs measurably change behaviour [4]. Here, that effect is used to filter out reflexive entries while still allowing genuine ones.

5.4 Glanceability.

The list is designed to be read in seconds. Items are short, the layout is predictable, and the state of the list can be taken in at a glance. This supports the Read step, which depends on reading the list taking almost no effort [3].

5.5 Unlocks and feedback.

Rewards that depend on completing a task can undermine intrinsic motivation, while positive feedback tends to increase it [11]. Welm's unlocks are therefore designed as acknowledgement, not prizes. They show that the practice is being kept up, and nothing in the app is withheld behind them.

6. Growth

6.1 Progressive functionality.

Welm starts with the minimum needed to run the loop, and further functionality becomes available over time. This follows the "training wheels" approach to interface design. New users of a word processor whose advanced functions were blocked learned faster and performed better than users of the full system, who spent almost a quarter of their time recovering from errors [12].

6.2 Habit formation.

The method is designed to become automatic rather than maintained by effort. The daily loop gives a consistent cue, a short routine and a visible result, which are the conditions under which automaticity develops [10].
