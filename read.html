Welmströmung

Welmströmung (n.) /ˈvɛlmˌʃtrøːmʊŋ/ A steady current of deliberate action.

1. Premises in productivity

Welmströmung is based on several findings about how people typically judge effort, choose between actions and direct attention. None of these findings are new; Welmströmung lies in how they are combined and applied.

1.1 Judgments are relative.

People judge magnitude against reference points, not in isolation. Introducing anchors above or below a series of stimuli shifts how every item in the series is rated [1]. Subjective effort appears to work similarly. One account proposes that the felt cost of a task reflects its value compared with the next-best use of the same mental resources [2]. Welm assumes the same holds within a list: how hard a task looks depends partly on what is shown next to it.

1.2 People avoid demand, and small frictions matter.

Given a choice between lines of action, people consistently prefer the less demanding one, often without being aware of it [3]. Small amounts of procedural friction also have large effects. In a field experiment, helping families complete a complex aid application substantially increased submission rates, while giving information alone had no significant effect [4]. Friction can work in either direction: removing it makes an action more likely, and adding it makes an action less likely.

1.3 Salient items capture attention.

Attention is drawn to whatever stands out most, even when it is irrelevant. In visual search, an item with a unique colour pulls attention away from a known target, and this persists with practice [5]. In an interface, the most visually distinct element is the one most likely to be acted on.

2. The to-do list problem

People spend little effort creating tasks for to-do lists, and many of those tasks are never completed [6]. The same research found that people are not poor at prioritising. Rather, the difficulty lies in the effort needed to keep a task list working [6].

Tasks accumulate because adding them is fast and cheap, while completing them is slow and costly.

As a list grows, older items sink out of view, and it becomes a record of obligations rather than a plan of action. The resulting cognitive cost is significant: unfulfilled goals stay active in the mind and interfere with unrelated tasks [7].

A to-do list works when every item in focus was deliberately chosen. It fails when it becomes a place where items accumulate by default.

3. Welm mechanisms

Welmströmung turns the three premises in productivity (see Section 1) into three mechanisms, each encapsulated by the process rather than offered as additional steps or actions to take.

3.1 Completion earns the capacity of addition.

The focus set must have a fixed capacity. A new task can only be promoted into focus once an existing one has been completed or removed. Promoting becomes a decision rather than a reflex, addressing the growth problem in Section 2 and keeping the number of open goals low [7]. It also changes where pressure falls. In an unbounded list, the backlog's weight is spread evenly. In Welmströmung, the remaining items press on the task in focus, because finishing it is what makes room for other priorities. The demand-avoidance tendency in 1.2 is directed toward completion instead of away from it.

3.2 Choice within a set.

Tasks are presented in small sets rather than as one continuous list, and the user must be able to choose freely from them. Decision time increases with the number of alternatives [8]. Choice overload, where large option sets lead to deferral and dissatisfaction, is contested as a general effect. A meta-analysis found it depends on specific conditions, including task difficulty, preference uncertainty and a goal of minimising effort [9]. Choosing what to do next often meets those conditions. Small sets reduce the cost of each choice, and comparison within a set (1.1) usually makes at least one task look manageable.

3.3 Reduced distraction.

Focus tasks are visually separated from the rest of the list and always appear first. Everything else is subdued. This uses attentional capture (1.3) deliberately: the most salient element on screen is always the one the user has chosen to work on [5].

4. The practice

Welmströmung is a practice comprising a simple loop with specific priorities.

Focus tasks. When engaging the system, the most salient information should first be the current set of focus tasks. This should be limited to 2-3 tasks. All sessions of Welmströmung begin with this reminder.

Adding. Capturing tasks should be fast and streamlined, so the user can move on to their next goals. Curating, editing and setting up should not happen at the capture stage.

Prioritising. Promoting tasks into empty slots is the prioritisation process. Since promoting is the mechanism for deciding what to do, it should be fluid and easy to grasp spatially.

In a study of real-world habit formation, behaviours repeated daily in a consistent context became more automatic along an asymptotic curve. The time to reach a plateau varied widely between people [10]. The key insight for this loop is to make it repetitive and simple: a consistent cue, a short routine and a visible result.

5. Design notes

5.1 A lean visual system for minimal stimulation.

Every visual element competes for attention with task completion, so the system's default is to embody a restrained palette, a simple type scale, and fewer variables (contrast and, to some extent, scale) to eliminate competing modes of focus.

5.2 Highlighting and visibility of focus tasks.

High visibility is reserved for giving focus tasks maximum contrast. Not even primary CTAs compete here. To ensure harmony with sticky elements, cubic-bezier gradient overlays are combined with custom opacity tuning.

5.3 Friction.

Friction is removed from the aspects of Welmströmung that promote task completion: completing tasks and promoting tasks into empty slots. Friction is added to the aspects that break its principles: de-prioritising tasks, attempting to skip tasks, or attempting to complete tasks that have not been prioritised. Small procedural costs measurably change behaviour [4]. Here, that effect is used to steer people towards effective behaviour.

5.4 Progressive functionality.

Welm starts with the minimum needed to run the loop, and further functionality becomes available over time. This follows the "training wheels" approach to interface design. New users of a word processor whose advanced functions were blocked learned faster and performed better than users of the full system, who spent almost a quarter of their time recovering from errors [12].

5.5 Unlocks and feedback.

Rewards that depend on completing a task can undermine intrinsic motivation, while positive feedback tends to increase it [11]. Welm's unlocks are therefore designed as acknowledgement, not prizes. They show that the practice is being kept up. Functionality is staged to keep the start simple (5.4), but nothing is withheld as a reward.
